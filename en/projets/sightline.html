Personal project, live web app, built by directing AI agents

Sightline: analysing a Counter‑Strike 2 match like a professional staff

In Counter-Strike 2, every match can be recorded in a demo file that holds everything: positions, shots, grenades, money. Sightline turns these files into a 2D replay, statistics and preparation tools for a team. I built the first version in three days by directing AI agents, then grew it until it covers most of a professional tool: I decide what to build and how to check it, the agents write the code. I then linked it to n8n, so that demos arrive on their own and the team gets a debrief on Discord.

The need

To improve at Counter-Strike you have to review your matches: where the players were, why a round was lost, what the opponent usually does. The tools teams use for this are online services, most of them paid. I wanted my own tool, hosted on my server, covering the whole need: import a demo, replay it, analyse it and prepare the next match.

Vibe coding, with a method

Vibe coding means describing what you want in plain language and letting an AI write the code. It is fast, but the risk is well known: you end up with an app nobody understands, full of invisible mistakes. My job was to manage these agents the way you would manage a team of developers.

  1. Specify before coding. Each major part (import and replay, statistics, grenades, heatmaps, annotations, team space, scouting) first gets a design document that sets the expected result. With the audits, that makes 21 documents.
  2. Split and assign. Twenty implementation plans break the work into small tasks, handed one at a time to agents, with a review between tasks.
  3. Verify. 92 automated test files, tests on real match demos, full user journeys played in a browser and scripted “beta tester” journeys.
  4. Compare and fix. I had the 57 features of a professional product on the market, Skybox EDGE, inventoried, then the gap split into thirteen sub-projects. Result: 42 features covered, 11 partial, 2 missing, 2 out of reach (they need game servers). Ergonomics and speed audits follow every major step.
  5. Operate. The app runs in a Docker container on my server, over HTTPS, with mandatory sign-in and a nightly database backup kept for 14 days.

What the app does

  • Import a folder of demos, a file dropped into the window or a FACEIT link; n8n, in service since 23 October, fetches practice-match demos on its own.
  • Replay every round in 2D, with smokes pierced by grenades and bullets, synchronised voice comms and a group review where a leader drives the replay for everyone.
  • Analyse the round-by-round win probability, the economy, heatmaps, highlights (clutches, multi-kills) and player leaderboards.
  • Prepare for an opponent: a scouting sheet across several demos, rounds to review, a veto simulator with predictions, a match prep file, a team playbook and a calendar.
  • Debrief like a staff: where the match was decided compared with usual form, three points to work on, five rounds to review and goals tracked from one match to the next.
  • Learn a role by following a professional player step by step, with an AI coach that explains a round, and ask questions to an assistant that reads the match data without being able to change it.
  • Understand mistakes: for each death, where the player was looking when the duel started and whether they had help; grenades bought but never thrown; the opponent's slow rounds broken into phases. In the replay, the list of a round's grenades, with what is needed to replay them in game.
  • Take notes on a map, a match, an opponent or a strategy, for yourself or for the team, on the site as well as in the mobile app.
  • Get notified: live team notifications, also on Discord, when a match is ready or commented on; for one of the team's matches, a three-line debrief written by the AI.
  • Keep Sightline in the pocket: a mobile app for the player, with the same accounts and data.

Making AI reliable: a checked scouting report

Sightline can write a scouting report on an opponent, as an analyst would. The risk of AI-written text is well known: it can invent a figure or swap two bomb sites with great confidence. So I had the report built so that the software computes the facts, the AI only writes, and everything is checked before display.

To know whether this check holds, I had it measured on a test bench: known errors are injected into real reports (shifted figure, wrong percentage, non-existent fact, swapped sites), and we count what gets caught.

  • CountedNumber errors caught by the automatic check: from 81% to 89-92% after tightening the rules, with no false alarm on an intact report.
  • CountedMeaning errors (swapped sites or players): the number check sees none, the judge catches 10 out of 12, with 2 alerts on the intact report.

Automating around the app with n8n

The team plays its practice matches on PRACC, the platform where it organises them and finds their demos. I wanted those demos to reach Sightline on their own, and the team to get a Discord debrief of every new match without opening the site.

I put n8n at the centre. Sightline does not know about n8n: it only exposes protected entry points, and all the integration logic lives in workflows that read as diagrams, with the run history. Adding a destination, such as another messaging app or a spreadsheet, does not require touching the app's code. The core (API keys, webhooks, workflows W0 to W2) followed the same method as the rest, on 22 October 2026: specification, a nine-task plan, a review after each task, then a review of the whole before going to production. The extension, the form and the debrief fixes were added on the 23rd, tested, but without that final review. The whole chain has been in service since 23 October: the extension runs a round every 30 minutes and n8n posts to the team's channel.

Four workflows

  • W1, from PRACC to Sightline. Every 30 minutes, a Chrome extension reads the list of the team's demos on PRACC, with the session open in the browser, and sends it to n8n. n8n keeps only new, available demos, asks Sightline which ones it already has, then has at most three imported per round, filed with their date and opponent. No imports during the server's nightly backup.
  • W2, from Sightline to Discord. Sightline sends a signed event when a demo arrives, when a match is analysed for the first time or when an import fails, and n8n posts the message to the team's channel. For one of our team's matches, n8n reads the debrief and statistics in Sightline, then Claude Haiku turns them into three lines from our point of view: the key fact, what worked, what cost us rounds, instructed to use only figures present in the data.
  • W4, an import form. An n8n page protected by a team login, where a teammate without an account drops a demo, up to 1 GB. It follows the same path, without duplicates, and n8n keeps neither the file nor the run.
  • W0, monitoring. If a workflow crashes, n8n posts the workflow name, the step and the error message on Discord.

What Sightline exposes, and how it is protected

  • API keys with limited rights: import demos, or read matches, players and teams, never settings or accounts. Each key is stored as a hash, shown only once and revocable; an action outside its rights is refused.
  • Signed events: each delivery carries a JWT signed with the webhook's secret and valid for 5 minutes, which n8n checks itself. Three attempts, a delivery log, and the destination address checked again before each delivery, to refuse internal addresses.
  • Imports without duplicates: a demo is imported only once per identifier, a file already present is attached to its demo, and a broken demo is dropped at the third failure instead of coming back every 30 minutes.
  • An isolated n8n: in its own container, reachable over HTTPS only, without system commands, with encrypted credentials. The exported workflows contain no secret: I paste every key into n8n myself.

What going live taught me

  • PRACC ties the session to the IP address. Copied to the server, it was refused, and PRACC signed me out. Hence the extension: reading happens in my browser, the session never leaves Chrome, and n8n keeps every decision.
  • The first debrief on a real match revealed three defects, fixed the same day. n8n 2's AI agent no longer ran the HTTP tools I had given it: it is replaced by a single call to Claude, one call instead of three or four. The model used up its answer thinking and returned an empty summary. And the summary read the debrief of the match's first team, here the opponent, instead of ours.

What I take from it, and the limits

  • My contribution is not typing the code. It lies in the specification, the splitting, the verification and the operations. I can explain the architecture and every choice, not recite every line.
  • A dependency to watch. Reading demos relies on the demoparser2 library, which has to follow every game update. The code is organised so that a single file depends on it.
  • AI keeps its limits. The check cannot see an invented number that happens to match a real value, and the judge is sometimes wrong. That is why the text stays tied to its facts and the remarks stay visible.
  • Automation that depends on an open browser. PRACC only accepts the session from the address where it was opened: the extension runs only while Chrome is open on my PC. Nothing is lost, it catches up on missed demos at start-up, three per round, and a teammate can install the same extension to take over.
