Sightline: analysing a Counter-Strike 2 match like a professional staff

In Counter-Strike 2, every match can be recorded in a demo file that holds everything: positions, shots, grenades, money. Sightline turns these files into a 2D replay, statistics and preparation tools for a team. I built the first version in three days by directing AI agents, then grew it until it covers most of a professional tool: I decide what to build and how to check it, the agents write the code.

The need

To improve at Counter-Strike you have to review your matches: where the players were, why a round was lost, what the opponent usually does. The tools teams use for this are online services, most of them paid. I wanted my own tool, hosted on my server, covering the whole need: import a demo, replay it, analyse it and prepare the next match.

Vibe coding, with a method

Vibe coding means describing what you want in plain language and letting an AI write the code. It is fast, but the risk is well known: you end up with an app nobody understands, full of invisible mistakes. My job was to manage these agents the way you would manage a team of developers.

  1. Specify before coding. Each major part (import and replay, statistics, grenades, heatmaps, annotations, team space, scouting) first gets a design document that sets the expected result. With the audits, that makes 19 documents.
  2. Split and assign. Eighteen implementation plans break the work into small tasks, handed one at a time to agents, with a review between tasks.
  3. Verify. 81 automated test files, tests on real match demos, full user journeys played in a browser and scripted “beta tester” journeys.
  4. Compare and fix. I had the 57 features of a professional product on the market, Skybox EDGE, inventoried, then the gap split into thirteen sub-projects. Result: 42 features covered, 11 partial, 2 missing, 2 out of reach (they need game servers). Ergonomics and speed audits follow every major step.
  5. Operate. The app runs in a Docker container on my server, over HTTPS, with mandatory sign-in and a nightly database backup kept for 14 days.

What the app does

  • Import a folder of demos, a file dropped into the window or a FACEIT link.
  • Replay every round in 2D, with smokes pierced by grenades and bullets, synchronised voice comms and a group review where a leader drives the replay for everyone.
  • Analyse the round-by-round win probability, the economy, heatmaps, highlights (clutches, multi-kills) and player leaderboards.
  • Prepare for an opponent: a scouting sheet across several demos, rounds to review, a veto simulator with predictions, a match prep file, a team playbook and a calendar.
  • Debrief like a staff: where the match was decided compared with usual form, three points to work on, five rounds to review and goals tracked from one match to the next.
  • Learn a role by following a professional player step by step, with an AI coach that explains a round, and ask questions to an assistant that reads the match data without being able to change it.
  • Understand mistakes: for each death, where the player was looking when the duel started and whether they had help; grenades bought but never thrown; the opponent's slow rounds broken into phases. In the replay, the list of a round's grenades, with what is needed to replay them in game.
  • Get notified: live team notifications, also on Discord, when a match is ready or commented on.
  • Keep Sightline in the pocket: a mobile app for the player, with the same accounts and data.

Making AI reliable: a checked scouting report

Sightline can write a scouting report on an opponent, as an analyst would. The risk of AI-written text is well known: it can invent a figure or swap two bomb sites with great confidence. So I had the report built so that the software computes the facts, the AI only writes, and everything is checked before display.

To know whether this check holds, I had it measured on a test bench: known errors are injected into real reports (shifted figure, wrong percentage, non-existent fact, swapped sites), and we count what gets caught.

  • CountedNumber errors caught by the automatic check: from 81% to 89-92% after tightening the rules, with no false alarm on an intact report.
  • CountedMeaning errors (swapped sites or players): the number check sees none, the judge catches 10 out of 12, with 2 alerts on the intact report.

What I take from it, and the limits

  • My contribution is not typing the code. It lies in the specification, the splitting, the verification and the operations. I can explain the architecture and every choice, not recite every line.
  • A dependency to watch. Reading demos relies on the demoparser2 library, which has to follow every game update. The code is organised so that a single file depends on it.
  • AI keeps its limits. The check cannot see an invented number that happens to match a real value, and the judge is sometimes wrong. That is why the text stays tied to its facts and the remarks stay visible.
