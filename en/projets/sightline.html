Sightline: analysing a Counter-Strike 2 match like a professional staff

In Counter-Strike 2, every match can be recorded in a demo file that holds everything: positions, shots, grenades, money. Sightline turns these files into a 2D replay, statistics and preparation tools for a team. I built it in three days by directing AI agents: I decided what to build and how to check it, the agents wrote the code.

The need

To improve at Counter-Strike you have to review your matches: where the players were, why a round was lost, what the opponent usually does. The tools teams use for this are online services, most of them paid. I wanted my own tool, hosted on my server, covering the whole need: import a demo, replay it, analyse it and prepare the next match.

Vibe coding, with a method

Vibe coding means describing what you want in plain language and letting an AI write the code. It is fast, but the risk is well known: you end up with an app nobody understands, full of invisible mistakes. My job was to manage these agents the way you would manage a team of developers.

  1. Specify before coding. Each major part (import and replay, statistics, grenades, heatmaps, annotations, team space, scouting) first gets a design document that sets the expected result. There are 13 of them.
  2. Split and assign. Two implementation plans break the work into small tasks, handed one at a time to agents, with a review between tasks.
  3. Verify. 32 automated test files, tests on real match demos and full user journeys played in a browser.
  4. Compare and fix. Audits measure the tool against products on the market and against a player's journey; their proposals are then applied.
  5. Operate. The app runs in a Docker container on my server, over HTTPS, with mandatory sign-in and a nightly database backup kept for 14 days.

What the app does

  • Import a folder of demos, a file dropped into the window or a FACEIT link.
  • Replay every round in 2D, with smokes pierced by grenades and bullets, synchronised voice comms and a group review where a leader drives the replay for everyone.
  • Analyse the round-by-round win probability, the economy, heatmaps, highlights (clutches, multi-kills) and player leaderboards.
  • Prepare for an opponent: a scouting report across several demos, a veto simulator, a team stratbook and a calendar.
  • Learn a role by following a professional player step by step, and ask questions to an AI assistant that reads the match data without being able to change it.

What I take from it, and the limits

  • My contribution is not typing the code. It lies in the specification, the splitting, the verification and the operations. I can explain the architecture and every choice, not recite every line.
  • A dependency to watch. Reading demos relies on the demoparser2 library, which has to follow every game update. The code is organised so that a single file depends on it.
  • No usage figures published here. The screenshots show public professional matches, not a team's data.
